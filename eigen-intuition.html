    §5.1 · Eigenvectors and eigenvalues

    Most vectors get knocked off their line

    Multiplying by a matrix moves a vector, and usually turns it as well. Spin x around and watch Ax. For a few special directions, Ax lands back on the line through x. Those directions are the eigenvectors.

    Matrix A

    Notice

    • The dashed blue line is the line through x. The short orange drop shows how far Ax lands off that line.
    • When the drop shrinks to nothing, Ax = λx. The number λ says how much A stretched x along its own line.
    • Turn on “where every direction goes”. Along an eigenline the grey connector points straight in or out; everywhere else it cuts sideways.
    • The rotation preset turns every direction, so it has no real eigenvectors. Scaling by 2 turns none of them.

    What this builds

    Definition (§5.1). An eigenvector of $A$ is a nonzero $\mathbf x$ with $A\mathbf x=\lambda\mathbf x$ for some scalar $\lambda$; that $\lambda$ is the eigenvalue it corresponds to.

    Checking is easy. To test a proposed eigenvector $\mathbf v$, compute $A\mathbf v$ and ask whether it is a multiple of $\mathbf v$ (§5.1 Example 2). Finding eigenvectors is the hard part; stop 3 shows how.

    Why nonzero? $A\mathbf 0=\lambda\mathbf 0$ for every $\lambda$, so allowing $\mathbf x=\mathbf 0$ would make every number an eigenvalue.

    §5.1 · Eigenspaces

    On its own line, A can only stretch

    Once a vector sits on an eigenline, A can't turn it. All A can do there is multiply by λ. This matrix has eigenvectors v₁ = (2, 1) and v₂ = (−1, 1), and you choose what it does to each line. Watch what happens to the F.

    Notice

    • Points on the v₁-line stay on it and slide by the factor λ₁. Same for v₂. Everything else moves as a mix of those two slides.
    • A negative λ reverses direction along its line, so the F flips over.
    • Set a λ to 0. That whole line collapses to the origin, the F flattens, and A stops being invertible.
    • The F's area is multiplied by |λ₁λ₂|, which is |det A|.

    What this builds

    Eigenspace (§5.1). The eigenvectors for $\lambda$, together with $\mathbf 0$, form $\operatorname{Nul}(A-\lambda I)$, a subspace. Here each eigenspace is a whole line.

    Zero as an eigenvalue. $\lambda=0$ means $A\mathbf x=\mathbf 0$ for some $\mathbf x\neq\mathbf 0$, so $A$ is not invertible. The Invertible Matrix Theorem gains statement (r): $0$ is not an eigenvalue of $A$ (§5.2).

    Theorem 1. The eigenvalues of a triangular matrix are its diagonal entries. For $A=\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\0&d\end{bmatrix}$, the matrix $A-aI$ has a zero first column, so it sends $\mathbf e_1$ to $\mathbf 0$.

    Repeating A. On an eigenline, applying $A$ $k$ times multiplies by $\lambda^k$. That is why $\mathbf x_k=\lambda^k\mathbf x_0$ solves $\mathbf x_{k+1}=A\mathbf x_k$ when $\mathbf x_0$ is an eigenvector.

    §5.2 · The characteristic equation

    Hunting λ: watch A − λI flatten the plane

    Spinning vectors doesn't scale to bigger matrices, so turn the question around. λ is an eigenvalue exactly when (A − λI)x = 0 has a nonzero solution, and that happens exactly when A − λI squashes the plane flat. A determinant measures the squash.

    Matrix A

    Notice

    • The shaded parallelogram is the unit square after A − λI. Its area is |det(A − λI)|.
    • At an eigenvalue the parallelogram collapses to a segment. Two different vectors land on the same point, so some nonzero vector goes to 0. That vector spans the eigenspace.
    • For a 2×2 matrix, det(A − λI) = λ² − (trace A)λ + det A. A degree-2 polynomial has at most two roots, so there are at most two eigenvalues.
    • Shear: the graph only touches 0 (a double root). Rotation: the graph never reaches 0, so there are no real eigenvalues.

    What this builds

    Characteristic equation (§5.2). A scalar $\lambda$ is an eigenvalue of $A$ if and only if $\det(A-\lambda I)=0$.

    Why a determinant? $|\det(A-\lambda I)|$ is the area scale factor of $A-\lambda I$ (§3.3). Area $0$ means the plane is flattened, so $A-\lambda I$ is not invertible (Theorem 3a), so $(A-\lambda I)\mathbf x=\mathbf 0$ has a nontrivial solution.

    Multiplicity. For an $n\times n$ matrix the characteristic polynomial has degree $n$. The (algebraic) multiplicity of an eigenvalue is its multiplicity as a root. The shear's graph touches $0$ at $\lambda=1$ without crossing: a double root.

    Warning (§5.1). Once you know $\lambda$, row reducing $A-\lambda I$ finds the eigenvectors. Row reducing $A$ itself does not reveal eigenvalues.

    §5.3 · Diagonalization

    Diagonalize: split x into eigen-parts

    With enough eigenvectors to make a basis, every x is a mix c₁v₁ + c₂v₂. Then A only rescales each part: Ax = c₁λ₁v₁ + c₂λ₂v₂, and repeating it gives Aᵏx = c₁λ₁ᵏv₁ + c₂λ₂ᵏv₂. That is all A = PDP⁻¹ says.

    x → P⁻¹ → (c₁, c₂) → D → (λ₁c₁, λ₂c₂) → P → Ax

    What this builds

    Theorem 5 (The Diagonalization Theorem). An $n\times n$ matrix $A$ is diagonalizable if and only if it has $n$ linearly independent eigenvectors. In fact $A=PDP^{-1}$, with $D$ diagonal, exactly when the columns of $P$ are $n$ linearly independent eigenvectors and the diagonal entries of $D$ are their eigenvalues, in the same order.

    Powers (§5.3). $A^k=PD^kP^{-1}$, and $D^k$ raises each diagonal entry to the $k$th power. Read it right to left: find eigen-coordinates, scale each one by $\lambda^k$, convert back.

    Dynamical systems (§5.2). If $\mathbf x_0=c_1\mathbf v_1+c_2\mathbf v_2$, then $\mathbf x_k=c_1\lambda_1^k\mathbf v_1+c_2\lambda_2^k\mathbf v_2$.

    §5.3 · Theorems 2, 6 and 7

    When eigenlines collide

    Diagonalizing needs n independent eigenvectors. Distinct eigenvalues guarantee them. A repeated eigenvalue might leave you one short. Slide ε to push two eigenvalues together and watch their lines.

    Family

    Notice

    • Shear family: the eigenlines are the horizontal axis and the line through (1, ε). The angle between them shrinks as ε → 0, and det P = ε shrinks with it.
    • At ε = 0 the lines merge. λ = 2 has multiplicity 2 but only a one-dimensional eigenspace, so no invertible P exists.
    • Scaling family: at ε = 0 every direction is an eigenvector. The repeated eigenvalue does no harm here.
    • A repeated eigenvalue is a warning sign, not a verdict. Compare each eigenspace's dimension with the eigenvalue's multiplicity.

    What this builds

    Theorem 2. Eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues are linearly independent. A line can't be stretched by two different factors at once, so distinct $\lambda$'s live on different lines.

    Theorem 6. An $n\times n$ matrix with $n$ distinct eigenvalues is diagonalizable. That condition is sufficient, not necessary: the scaling family at $\varepsilon=0$ is diagonalizable with a repeated eigenvalue.

    Theorem 7. (a) Each eigenspace has dimension at most the multiplicity of its eigenvalue. (b) $A$ is diagonalizable exactly when the eigenspace dimensions add up to $n$; equivalently, the characteristic polynomial factors completely into linear factors and each eigenspace's dimension equals its eigenvalue's multiplicity. (c) Put bases of the eigenspaces together and you get an eigenvector basis.

    §5.4 · Eigenvectors and linear transformations

    Same map, different coordinates

    A matrix is one description of a transformation, written in some coordinates. Pick a basis B = {b₁, b₂} and describe T(x) = Ax in B-coordinates: column j of [T]B says how to build T(bj) out of b₁ and b₂. Drag the basis vectors and the description changes while the map stays put.

    Notice

    • With the standard basis, [T]B is just A.
    • Every entry can change, but the trace, determinant and eigenvalues never do. Similar matrices share a characteristic polynomial.
    • Line both basis vectors up with eigenvectors and [T]B turns diagonal: T just stretches each basis vector.
    • Example 5's matrix has only one eigenline. The best you can do is triangular, with the eigenvalue −2 on the diagonal.

    What this builds

    The matrix for $T$ relative to $\mathcal B$ (§5.4). $[T(\mathbf x)]_{\mathcal B}=[T]_{\mathcal B}[\mathbf x]_{\mathcal B}$, where $[T]_{\mathcal B}=\begin{bmatrix}[T(\mathbf b_1)]_{\mathcal B}&\cdots&[T(\mathbf b_n)]_{\mathcal B}\end{bmatrix}$.

    For $T(\mathbf x)=A\mathbf x$ on $\mathbb R^n$, $[T]_{\mathcal B}=P^{-1}AP$ with $P=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf b_1&\cdots&\mathbf b_n\end{bmatrix}$. Every matrix representation of $\mathbf x\mapsto A\mathbf x$ is similar to $A$, and every matrix similar to $A$ is one of its representations.

    Theorem 8. If $A=PDP^{-1}$ with $D$ diagonal and $\mathcal B$ is formed from the columns of $P$, then $D$ is the $\mathcal B$-matrix for $\mathbf x\mapsto A\mathbf x$.

    Theorem 4. Similar matrices have the same characteristic polynomial, so the same eigenvalues with the same multiplicities. That is why the trace, determinant and eigenvalues in the panel never move. The converse fails: same eigenvalues does not mean similar.

    Beyond arrows. Eigenvectors make sense for any linear $T:V\to V$. For differentiation on $\mathbb P_2$ with $\mathcal B=\{1,t,t^2\}$, $[T]_{\mathcal B}=\begin{bmatrix}0&1&0\\0&0&2\\0&0&0\end{bmatrix}$ (§5.4 Example 3). It is triangular with only $0$ on the diagonal, and its eigenvectors are the nonzero constant polynomials, since $\tfrac{d}{dt}c=0\cdot c$.

    Review · §5.1–5.4

    The whole path, start to finish

    1. An eigenvector is a direction $A$ doesn't turn: $A\mathbf x=\lambda\mathbf x$ with $\mathbf x\neq\mathbf 0$. Stop 1
    2. The eigenvalue is the stretch along that line. $\lambda=0$ crushes the line to $\mathbf 0$, so $A$ isn't invertible. Stop 2
    3. Find $\lambda$ first: $\det(A-\lambda I)=0$ is the moment $A-\lambda I$ flattens space. Then solve $(A-\lambda I)\mathbf x=\mathbf 0$ for the eigenspace. Stop 3
    4. With $n$ independent eigenvectors, $A=PDP^{-1}$: switch to eigen-coordinates, stretch each axis, switch back. Powers come free: $A^k=PD^kP^{-1}$. Stop 4
    5. Distinct eigenvalues guarantee independent eigenvectors. A repeated eigenvalue might not supply enough, so compare each eigenspace's dimension with its multiplicity. Stop 5
    6. A matrix is one description of a map. Changing basis gives $P^{-1}AP$, a similar matrix with the same eigenvalues, and an eigenvector basis makes it diagonal. Stop 6
