§5.5 · pp. 304–309

Complex Eigenvalues

The characteristic polynomial of a real matrix can have complex roots. For a 2×2 matrix with real entries, complex roots mean no real direction stays on its own line. In a suitable basis, the matrix just rotates and scales.

The boxes

Definition: complex eigenvalue and eigenvector

A complex scalar $\lambda$ is an eigenvalue of $A$ if and only if $\det(A-\lambda I)=0$, that is, there is a nonzero $\mathbf x\in\mathbb C^n$ with $A\mathbf x=\lambda\mathbf x$. Then $\mathbf x$ is a (complex) eigenvector.

Real and imaginary parts

Every $\mathbf x\in\mathbb C^n$ splits as $\mathbf x=\operatorname{Re}\mathbf x+i\operatorname{Im}\mathbf x$, where $\operatorname{Re}\mathbf x$ and $\operatorname{Im}\mathbf x$ are in $\mathbb R^n$. Conjugation passes through products: $\overline{r\mathbf x}=\bar r\,\bar{\mathbf x}$, $\overline{B\mathbf x}=\bar B\,\bar{\mathbf x}$, $\overline{BC}=\bar B\,\bar C$.

Conjugate pairs (when $A$ is real)

If $A$ is real and $A\mathbf x=\lambda\mathbf x$, then $A\bar{\mathbf x}=\overline{A\mathbf x}=\bar\lambda\bar{\mathbf x}$. So complex eigenvalues come in pairs $a\pm bi$, and their eigenvectors are conjugates of each other.

The building block (Example 6)

If $C=\begin{bmatrix}a&-b\\ b&a\end{bmatrix}$ with $a,b$ real and not both zero, then $\lambda=a\pm bi$, and with $r=|\lambda|=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}$, $$C=r\begin{bmatrix}\cos\varphi&-\sin\varphi\\ \sin\varphi&\cos\varphi\end{bmatrix},$$ so $C$ rotates by the angle $\varphi$ (the argument of $a+bi$) and then scales by $r$.

Theorem 9

Let $A$ be a real $2\times2$ matrix with a complex eigenvalue $\lambda=a-bi$ ($b\ne0$) and an associated eigenvector $\mathbf v\in\mathbb C^2$. Then $$A=PCP^{-1},\quad P=\begin{bmatrix}\operatorname{Re}\mathbf v&\operatorname{Im}\mathbf v\end{bmatrix},\quad C=\begin{bmatrix}a&-b\\ b&a\end{bmatrix}.$$

Method: find the rotation hidden in $A$

  1. Solve $\lambda^2-(\operatorname{tr}A)\lambda+\det A=0$. A negative discriminant gives $\lambda=a\pm bi$.
  2. Take the root with the minus sign, $\lambda=a-bi$. This is the one Theorem 9 uses.
  3. Find an eigenvector from one row of $A-\lambda I$. You can skip row reduction because the two rows are complex multiples of each other. If a row reads $[\,p\ \ q\,]$, then $\mathbf v=\begin{bmatrix}-q\\ p\end{bmatrix}$ works.
  4. Set $P=[\operatorname{Re}\mathbf v\ \ \operatorname{Im}\mathbf v]$ and $C=\begin{bmatrix}a&-b\\ b&a\end{bmatrix}$. Check $AP=PC$, which avoids computing $P^{-1}$.
  5. Read off the long-run behavior from $r=|\lambda|$: $r<1$ spirals in, $r=1$ stays on an ellipse, and $r>1$ spirals out.

Worked example (Examples 2 and 7)

Example$A=\begin{bmatrix}.5&-.6\\ .75&1.1\end{bmatrix}$

$\det(A-\lambda I)=\lambda^2-1.6\lambda+1=0$, so $\lambda=.8\pm.6i$. Take $\lambda=.8-.6i$: $$A-\lambda I=\begin{bmatrix}-.3+.6i&-.6\\ .75&.3+.6i\end{bmatrix}.$$ Row 2 gives $.75x_1=(-.3-.6i)x_2$. With $x_2=5$ this gives $\mathbf v_1=\begin{bmatrix}-2-4i\\5\end{bmatrix}$, so $$P=\begin{bmatrix}-2&-4\\5&0\end{bmatrix},\qquad C=P^{-1}AP=\begin{bmatrix}.8&-.6\\ .6&.8\end{bmatrix}.$$ Since $|\lambda|^2=.64+.36=1$, $C$ is a pure rotation. Points $\mathbf x_k=A^k\mathbf x_0$ go around an ellipse rather than a circle, because the axes given by $P$'s columns are not perpendicular and have different lengths (Figure 1, p. 306).

Traps

  • Sign mismatch. Theorem 9 pairs $\lambda=a-bi$ with $C=\begin{bmatrix}a&-b\\ b&a\end{bmatrix}$. If you use $a+bi$ with the same $P$ recipe, $AP=PC$ fails.
  • Column order. $P$ is $[\operatorname{Re}\mathbf v\ \ \operatorname{Im}\mathbf v]$, with the real part first.
  • "No real eigenvalues" ≠ "no eigenvalues." Over $\mathbb C$, an $n\times n$ matrix always has $n$ eigenvalues counting multiplicity.
  • Odd size. A real $3\times3$ matrix always has at least one real eigenvalue, because complex roots come in pairs. Example 8 has $.8\pm.6i$ and $1.07$.
  • $r=|\lambda|$ is $\sqrt{a^2+b^2}$, not $a$.

Self-check

TryFind $P$ and $C$ for $A=\begin{bmatrix}1&-2\\1&3\end{bmatrix}$. Does $A^k\mathbf x$ spiral in or out?

$\lambda^2-4\lambda+5=0\Rightarrow\lambda=2\pm i$. For $\lambda=2-i$, row 2 of $A-\lambda I$ is $[\,1\ \ 1+i\,]$, so $\mathbf v=\begin{bmatrix}-1-i\\1\end{bmatrix}$. $$P=\begin{bmatrix}-1&-1\\1&0\end{bmatrix},\quad C=\begin{bmatrix}2&-1\\1&2\end{bmatrix}.$$ Check the first column: $A\begin{bmatrix}-1\\1\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}-3\\2\end{bmatrix}=P\begin{bmatrix}2\\1\end{bmatrix}$. Since $r=\sqrt5>1$, it spirals out.

TryShow the eigenvalues of $\begin{bmatrix}a&-b\\b&a\end{bmatrix}$ are $a\pm bi$ with eigenvectors $\begin{bmatrix}1\\-i\end{bmatrix}$ and $\begin{bmatrix}1\\i\end{bmatrix}$. (Practice Problem, p. 309)

$\begin{bmatrix}a&-b\\b&a\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1\\-i\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}a+bi\\b-ai\end{bmatrix}=(a+bi)\begin{bmatrix}1\\-i\end{bmatrix}$, because $(a+bi)(-i)=b-ai$. The other eigenpair is its conjugate.

§6.1 · pp. 350–356

Inner Product, Length, and Orthogonality

Length, distance, angle and perpendicularity in $\mathbb R^n$ all come from the dot product $\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v=\mathbf u^T\mathbf v$. The idea that leads into the rest of the chapter is the orthogonal complement $W^\perp$.

The boxes

Inner product

$\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v=\mathbf u^T\mathbf v=u_1v_1+\cdots+u_nv_n$. The result is a scalar.

Theorem 1

For $\mathbf u,\mathbf v,\mathbf w\in\mathbb R^n$ and scalar $c$:

  1. $\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v=\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf u$
  2. $(\mathbf u+\mathbf v)\cdot\mathbf w=\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf w+\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf w$
  3. $(c\mathbf u)\cdot\mathbf v=c(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v)=\mathbf u\cdot(c\mathbf v)$
  4. $\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf u\ge0$, and $\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf u=0$ iff $\mathbf u=\mathbf 0$

Length, unit vectors, distance

$\|\mathbf v\|=\sqrt{\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf v}$, so $\|\mathbf v\|^2=\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf v$. Also $\|c\mathbf v\|=|c|\,\|\mathbf v\|$. Normalizing means $\mathbf u=\mathbf v/\|\mathbf v\|$, a unit vector pointing the same way as $\mathbf v$. $\operatorname{dist}(\mathbf u,\mathbf v)=\|\mathbf u-\mathbf v\|$.

Definition: orthogonal

$\mathbf u$ and $\mathbf v$ are orthogonal if $\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v=0$. The zero vector is orthogonal to every vector.

Theorem 2 · The Pythagorean Theorem

$\mathbf u$ and $\mathbf v$ are orthogonal if and only if $\|\mathbf u+\mathbf v\|^2=\|\mathbf u\|^2+\|\mathbf v\|^2$.

Orthogonal complement $W^\perp$

$W^\perp$ is the set of all $\mathbf z$ orthogonal to every vector in $W$.

  1. $\mathbf x\in W^\perp$ iff $\mathbf x$ is orthogonal to every vector in a spanning set of $W$.
  2. $W^\perp$ is a subspace of $\mathbb R^n$.

Theorem 3

For an $m\times n$ matrix $A$: $\;(\operatorname{Row}A)^\perp=\operatorname{Nul}A\;$ and $\;(\operatorname{Col}A)^\perp=\operatorname{Nul}A^T$.

Angles (optional)

$\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v=\|\mathbf u\|\,\|\mathbf v\|\cos\vartheta$.

Method: compute $W^\perp$

  1. Write a spanning set of $W$ as the rows of a matrix $A$.
  2. By Theorem 3, $W^\perp=(\operatorname{Row}A)^\perp=\operatorname{Nul}A$. Row reduce $A$ and solve $A\mathbf x=\mathbf 0$.
  3. Check the dimensions: $\dim W+\dim W^\perp=n$ (Practice Problem 3 from the rank theorem).

Worked examples (Examples 2 and 4)

ExampleNormalize $\mathbf v=(1,-2,2,0)$, and find $\operatorname{dist}\big((7,1),(3,2)\big)$.

$\|\mathbf v\|^2=1+4+4+0=9$, so $\mathbf u=\tfrac13\mathbf v=(\tfrac13,-\tfrac23,\tfrac23,0)$. To check, $\|\mathbf u\|^2=\tfrac19+\tfrac49+\tfrac49=1$.

$\mathbf u-\mathbf v=(4,-1)$, so the distance is $\sqrt{16+1}=\sqrt{17}$.

Traps

  • $\|\mathbf u+\mathbf v\|=\|\mathbf u\|+\|\mathbf v\|$ is false in general. It is the squares that add, and only when $\mathbf u\perp\mathbf v$.
  • $(\operatorname{Col}A)^\perp$ is $\operatorname{Nul}A^{T}$, not $\operatorname{Nul}A$. The two spaces don't even sit in the same $\mathbb R^k$ unless $m=n$.
  • When you test $\mathbf x\in W^\perp$, a spanning set is enough. You do not have to check every vector in $W$.
  • Scaling a vector before normalizing doesn't change the unit vector. Clear fractions first, as in Example 3.

Self-check

TryFind a basis for $W^\perp$ where $W=\operatorname{Span}\{(1,1,1)\}\subset\mathbb R^3$.

$W^\perp=\operatorname{Nul}[\,1\ 1\ 1\,]$, which is the plane $x_1+x_2+x_3=0$. A basis is $\{(-1,1,0),(-1,0,1)\}$. The dimensions check: $1+2=3$.

TryLet $\mathbf a=(-2,1)$, $\mathbf b=(-3,1)$. Compute $\frac{\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b}{\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf a}\mathbf a$. (Practice Problem 1)

$\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b=7$ and $\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf a=5$, so the answer is $\tfrac75\mathbf a=(-\tfrac{14}5,\tfrac75)$. This quantity is the projection of $\mathbf b$ onto $\mathbf a$, which returns in 6.2.

TryTrue or false: if $\mathbf x$ is orthogonal to every row of $A$, then $A\mathbf x=\mathbf 0$.

True. Each entry of $A\mathbf x$ is a row of $A$ dotted with $\mathbf x$. This is the proof of Theorem 3.

§6.2 · pp. 358–364

Orthogonal Sets

With a basis whose vectors are mutually perpendicular, you never need to row reduce to find coordinates: each weight is one dot product divided by another. Normalize the vectors as well and you get matrices with $U^TU=I$, which preserve lengths and angles.

The boxes

Definition: orthogonal set

$\{\mathbf u_1,\dots,\mathbf u_p\}$ is an orthogonal set if $\mathbf u_i\cdot\mathbf u_j=0$ whenever $i\ne j$.

Theorem 4

An orthogonal set $S$ of nonzero vectors in $\mathbb R^n$ is linearly independent, and so is a basis for $\operatorname{Span}S$.

Definition: orthogonal basis

An orthogonal basis for $W$ is a basis for $W$ that is also an orthogonal set.

Theorem 5

If $\{\mathbf u_1,\dots,\mathbf u_p\}$ is an orthogonal basis for $W$, then each $\mathbf y\in W$ satisfies $\mathbf y=c_1\mathbf u_1+\cdots+c_p\mathbf u_p$ with $$c_j=\frac{\mathbf y\cdot\mathbf u_j}{\mathbf u_j\cdot\mathbf u_j}.$$

Projection onto a line

For $\mathbf u\ne\mathbf 0$ and $L=\operatorname{Span}\{\mathbf u\}$: $$\hat{\mathbf y}=\operatorname{proj}_L\mathbf y=\frac{\mathbf y\cdot\mathbf u}{\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf u}\,\mathbf u,\qquad \mathbf z=\mathbf y-\hat{\mathbf y}\ \perp\ \mathbf u.$$ $\hat{\mathbf y}$ depends only on $L$, not on which $\mathbf u$ spans it. The distance from $\mathbf y$ to $L$ is $\|\mathbf y-\hat{\mathbf y}\|$.

Orthonormal sets

An orthonormal set is an orthogonal set of unit vectors. An orthonormal basis for $W$ is a basis that is an orthonormal set.

Theorem 6

An $m\times n$ matrix $U$ has orthonormal columns if and only if $U^TU=I$.

Theorem 7

If $U$ ($m\times n$) has orthonormal columns and $\mathbf x,\mathbf y\in\mathbb R^n$:

  1. $\|U\mathbf x\|=\|\mathbf x\|$
  2. $(U\mathbf x)\cdot(U\mathbf y)=\mathbf x\cdot\mathbf y$
  3. $(U\mathbf x)\cdot(U\mathbf y)=0$ iff $\mathbf x\cdot\mathbf y=0$

Orthogonal matrix

An orthogonal matrix is a square invertible $U$ with $U^{-1}=U^T$. Its columns are orthonormal, and so are its rows. Also $\det U=\pm1$ (Practice Problem 4).

Method: coordinates in an orthogonal basis

  1. Check every pair: $\mathbf u_i\cdot\mathbf u_j=0$. If any pair fails, Theorem 5 does not apply.
  2. For each $j$, compute $\mathbf y\cdot\mathbf u_j$ and $\mathbf u_j\cdot\mathbf u_j$.
  3. Divide to get $c_j$, then check by recombining.

Worked examples (Examples 2 and 3)

ExampleWrite $\mathbf y=(6,1,-8)$ in the basis $\mathbf u_1=(3,1,1)$, $\mathbf u_2=(-1,2,1)$, $\mathbf u_3=(-\tfrac12,-2,\tfrac72)$.

$\mathbf y\cdot\mathbf u_1=11,\ \mathbf u_1\cdot\mathbf u_1=11$; $\ \mathbf y\cdot\mathbf u_2=-12,\ \mathbf u_2\cdot\mathbf u_2=6$; $\ \mathbf y\cdot\mathbf u_3=-33,\ \mathbf u_3\cdot\mathbf u_3=\tfrac{33}2$. $$\mathbf y=\tfrac{11}{11}\mathbf u_1+\tfrac{-12}{6}\mathbf u_2+\tfrac{-33}{33/2}\mathbf u_3=\mathbf u_1-2\mathbf u_2-2\mathbf u_3.$$

ExampleProject $\mathbf y=(7,6)$ onto $\mathbf u=(4,2)$, and find the distance from $\mathbf y$ to $L$.

$\hat{\mathbf y}=\tfrac{40}{20}\mathbf u=(8,4)$ and $\mathbf z=\mathbf y-\hat{\mathbf y}=(-1,2)$. Check: $\hat{\mathbf y}\cdot\mathbf z=-8+8=0$. The distance is $\|\mathbf z\|=\sqrt5$.

Traps

  • Theorem 4 needs nonzero vectors. Adding $\mathbf 0$ keeps a set orthogonal but makes it dependent.
  • Theorem 5 gives correct weights only if $\mathbf y$ is in $W$. If it isn't, the same formula gives the projection (§6.3).
  • If $U$ has orthonormal columns but is not square, then $U^TU=I$ while $UU^T\ne I$. That case is not called an “orthogonal matrix.”
  • The term is orthogonal matrix, yet its columns must be orthonormal. Merely orthogonal columns are not enough.

Self-check

TryShow $\mathbf u_1=(2,-3)$, $\mathbf u_2=(6,4)$ is an orthogonal basis of $\mathbb R^2$, then write $\mathbf x=(9,-7)$ in it. (Exercise 7)

$\mathbf u_1\cdot\mathbf u_2=12-12=0$, and both vectors are nonzero, so by Theorem 4 they form a basis. $c_1=\tfrac{18+21}{13}=3$ and $c_2=\tfrac{54-28}{52}=\tfrac12$, so $\mathbf x=3\mathbf u_1+\tfrac12\mathbf u_2$. Check: $(6,-9)+(3,2)=(9,-7)$.

TryRedo the projection of $\mathbf y=(7,6)$ using $\mathbf u=(2,1)$ instead. (Practice Problem 2)

$\tfrac{20}{5}(2,1)=(8,4)$, the same answer as before, because the projection depends only on the line $L$.

TryWhy does $\det U=\pm1$ for a square $U$ with orthonormal columns?

$U^TU=I\Rightarrow\det(U^T)\det U=(\det U)^2=1$.

§6.3 · pp. 367–372

Orthogonal Projections

Projection onto a line extends to projection onto any subspace $W$. Every $\mathbf y$ splits uniquely into a part in $W$ and a part perpendicular to $W$, and the part in $W$ is the closest point of $W$ to $\mathbf y$. Least squares (§6.5) depends on this.

The boxes

Theorem 8 · The Orthogonal Decomposition Theorem

Let $W$ be a subspace of $\mathbb R^n$. Each $\mathbf y\in\mathbb R^n$ can be written uniquely as $\mathbf y=\hat{\mathbf y}+\mathbf z$ with $\hat{\mathbf y}\in W$ and $\mathbf z\in W^\perp$. If $\{\mathbf u_1,\dots,\mathbf u_p\}$ is any orthogonal basis of $W$, $$\hat{\mathbf y}=\frac{\mathbf y\cdot\mathbf u_1}{\mathbf u_1\cdot\mathbf u_1}\mathbf u_1+\cdots+\frac{\mathbf y\cdot\mathbf u_p}{\mathbf u_p\cdot\mathbf u_p}\mathbf u_p,\qquad \mathbf z=\mathbf y-\hat{\mathbf y}.$$ $\hat{\mathbf y}=\operatorname{proj}_W\mathbf y$ does not depend on which orthogonal basis you use.

Property

If $\mathbf y\in W=\operatorname{Span}\{\mathbf u_1,\dots,\mathbf u_p\}$, then $\operatorname{proj}_W\mathbf y=\mathbf y$.

Theorem 9 · The Best Approximation Theorem

$\hat{\mathbf y}=\operatorname{proj}_W\mathbf y$ is the closest point in $W$ to $\mathbf y$: $$\|\mathbf y-\hat{\mathbf y}\|<\|\mathbf y-\mathbf v\|\quad\text{for all }\mathbf v\in W,\ \mathbf v\ne\hat{\mathbf y}.$$ So the distance from $\mathbf y$ to $W$ is $\|\mathbf y-\hat{\mathbf y}\|$.

Theorem 10

If $\{\mathbf u_1,\dots,\mathbf u_p\}$ is an orthonormal basis for $W$ and $U=[\mathbf u_1\ \cdots\ \mathbf u_p]$, then $$\operatorname{proj}_W\mathbf y=(\mathbf y\cdot\mathbf u_1)\mathbf u_1+\cdots+(\mathbf y\cdot\mathbf u_p)\mathbf u_p=UU^T\mathbf y.$$

The two products

$U^TU\mathbf x=I_p\mathbf x=\mathbf x$ (Theorem 6), and $UU^T\mathbf y=\operatorname{proj}_W\mathbf y$ (Theorem 10). If $U$ is square, $W=\mathbb R^n$ and $UU^T=I$ as well.

Method: decompose $\mathbf y$ and find its distance to $W$

  1. First check the basis is orthogonal. If it is not, the formula gives a wrong answer, and you have to orthogonalize first (Gram–Schmidt, §6.4).
  2. Compute $\hat{\mathbf y}$ term by term. Each term is the projection onto one line $\operatorname{Span}\{\mathbf u_j\}$ (Figure 3, p. 369).
  3. Set $\mathbf z=\mathbf y-\hat{\mathbf y}$, then check $\mathbf z\cdot\mathbf u_j=0$ for each $j$.
  4. The distance from $\mathbf y$ to $W$ is $\|\mathbf z\|$.

Worked examples (Examples 2 and 4)

Example$\mathbf u_1=(2,5,-1)$, $\mathbf u_2=(-2,1,1)$, $\mathbf y=(1,2,3)$. Decompose $\mathbf y$.

$\mathbf u_1\cdot\mathbf u_2=-4+5-1=0$, so the basis is orthogonal. $$\hat{\mathbf y}=\tfrac{9}{30}\mathbf u_1+\tfrac36\mathbf u_2=\begin{bmatrix}-2/5\\2\\1/5\end{bmatrix},\qquad \mathbf z=\mathbf y-\hat{\mathbf y}=\begin{bmatrix}7/5\\0\\14/5\end{bmatrix}.$$ Check: $\mathbf z\cdot\mathbf u_1=\tfrac{14}5-\tfrac{14}5=0$ and $\mathbf z\cdot\mathbf u_2=-\tfrac{14}5+\tfrac{14}5=0$.

ExampleDistance from $\mathbf y=(-1,-5,10)$ to $W=\operatorname{Span}\{(5,-2,1),(1,2,-1)\}$.

$\hat{\mathbf y}=\tfrac{15}{30}\mathbf u_1+\tfrac{-21}{6}\mathbf u_2=(-1,-8,4)$ and $\mathbf y-\hat{\mathbf y}=(0,3,6)$, so the distance is $\sqrt{45}=3\sqrt5$.

Traps

  • The projection formula requires an orthogonal basis. If you use a non-orthogonal basis, $\mathbf y-\hat{\mathbf y}$ will not be perpendicular to $W$, and the $\mathbf z\cdot\mathbf u_j$ check will catch it.
  • $\mathbf z=\mathbf y-\hat{\mathbf y}$ is $\operatorname{proj}_{W^\perp}\mathbf y$. The two projections always add to $\mathbf y$.
  • $UU^T$ is $n\times n$ (a projection), while $U^TU$ is $p\times p$ (the identity). Keep them apart.
  • For hand calculation, use formula (2) with an orthogonal basis. Formula (4) with unit vectors brings in square roots.

Self-check

Try$W=\operatorname{Span}\{(1,1,0),(-1,1,1)\}$ and $\mathbf y=(3,1,4)$. Find $\operatorname{proj}_W\mathbf y$ and the distance from $\mathbf y$ to $W$.

The basis is orthogonal: $-1+1+0=0$. Then $\hat{\mathbf y}=\tfrac42(1,1,0)+\tfrac23(-1,1,1)=(\tfrac43,\tfrac83,\tfrac23)$ and $\mathbf z=(\tfrac53,-\tfrac53,\tfrac{10}3)$. Check: $\mathbf z\cdot\mathbf u_1=0$ and $\mathbf z\cdot\mathbf u_2=0$. The distance is $\tfrac53\sqrt6$.

TryIf $\mathbf y\in W^\perp$, what is $\operatorname{proj}_W\mathbf y$?

$\mathbf 0$. Every $\mathbf y\cdot\mathbf u_j=0$, and the decomposition is $\mathbf y=\mathbf 0+\mathbf y$.

Try$U$ is $4\times2$ with orthonormal columns. What sizes are $U^TU$ and $UU^T$, and which one equals $I$?

$U^TU$ is $2\times2$ and equals $I_2$. $UU^T$ is $4\times4$ and is the projection onto $\operatorname{Col}U$, which is not $I_4$.
